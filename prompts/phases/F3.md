# F3: Why

Question: **why did it change: volume, mix, or rate?**

F3 is the only phase allowed to name a cause. The permission is conditional: a cause
counts only if the components add up to the change that F2 measured, with a residual
of zero.

If you cannot make it add up, the honest answer is "tidak dapat diidentifikasi dari
data ini". That is a valid finding. A plausible story that does not reconcile is not.

## Entry condition

F2 produced a headline change with a base. Take that exact number as the quantity to
be explained:

```
delta = revenue_q3 - revenue_q2 = -49.6m
```

Every component you produce must sum to this number. If it does not, the
decomposition is wrong, not the total.

If F2 found no material change, F3 still has work: show which components offset each
other, and close. Do not manufacture a cause for a flat period.

## Is the change real

Before explaining it, check the change is bigger than the usual movement.

Print the median absolute period-over-period change across all periods. If the delta
you are explaining is smaller than that median, the change is within normal
variation. Say so, record it, and close the phase.

Explaining noise is how a report ends up recommending an action against a movement
that would have reversed on its own next month.

## Step order

| # | Step | Produces |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | restate the delta, and the noise baseline | `delta=-49.6m median_abs_change=18.2m` |
| 2 | decompose into volume and rate | two components, plus residual |
| 3 | split rate further if quantity and price exist | volume, mix, price, plus residual |
| 4 | rank segments by contribution to the delta | EP per segment, Pareto set, `lainnya` |
| 5 | denominator audit, if the metric is a ratio | numerator change and denominator change |
| 6 | Simpson audit | sign check per large segment, standardised rate if flipped |

## Level A: two factors, always available

Revenue is orders times average order value. This split needs only what F1 already
validated.

```
volume effect = (orders_1 - orders_0) * aov_0
rate effect   = orders_1 * (aov_1 - aov_0)
residual      = delta - volume effect - rate effect
```

The residual is zero by construction. Print it anyway, because the reader needs to
see that it is zero and not take your word for it.

Use this level when the data has no quantity and no unit price column. Most
small-business exports do not.

## Level B: three factors, when quantity and price exist

```
volume effect = (units_1 - units_0) * avg_price_0
mix effect    = sum over items of (share_1_i - share_0_i) * (price_0_i - avg_price_0) * units_1
price effect  = sum over items of units_1_i * (price_1_i - price_0_i)
```

Edge cases:

- A new item has no baseline price. Set its baseline price to its current price, so
  its price effect is zero and its revenue lands in volume and mix.
- A discontinued item has no current volume. Set its current price to its baseline
  price, so its price effect is zero.

Validation: the three effects must equal the delta within `1e-4` of the delta. Print
the residual. If it is not zero, one of the three is computed on the wrong grain, and
you fix that before writing anything down.

State which level you used. Level A is not a worse answer, it is the answer the data
supports.

## Segment attribution

For each mutually exclusive segment in the chosen dimension:

```
EP_i = (value_1_i - value_0_i) / delta
```

- EP sums to 1.0 across the segments of one dimension. Print the sum; if it is not
  1.0, the segments overlap or a row was dropped.
- Sort by absolute contribution, descending.
- Report the smallest set whose absolute contributions reach 80 percent of the total
  absolute movement. Everything after that goes into `lainnya`.
- Pareto drivers plus `lainnya` must equal the delta exactly. Print both sides.

Size is not surprise. A large segment moving slightly is expected; a tiny segment
collapsing by 90 percent is surprising but is not the cause of anything at the total
level. Only report a segment as the driver when its contribution is large. Mention
the small surprising one as an anomaly, not as the reason.

## Denominator audit

Applies when the metric is a ratio: average order value, conversion, refund rate,
items per order.

Never report a ratio change without both sides printed:

```
aov=-6.2% revenue=-12.4% orders=-6.6%
```

A ratio can fall while its numerator rises, if the denominator grows faster. The
sentence you write must say which side moved: the numerator fell, or the denominator
expanded. Those are different business problems and different actions.

Rule of thumb to print: `delta_ratio ≈ delta_num/num_0 - delta_den/den_0`. If the
printed decomposition does not roughly reproduce the ratio change, one of the two
sides is measured on the wrong population.

## Simpson audit

Do this before you state a direction as the answer.

1. Take the two or three largest segments of the strongest dimension.
2. Compute the delta inside each one.
3. Compare signs with the aggregate delta.
4. If every sign matches, the aggregate direction is safe to state.
5. If a sign flips, the aggregate direction is a mix artifact. Report the subgroup
   rates and the mix shift. Do not report the aggregate as the cause.

When the sign flips, quantify it with a standardised rate: reweight each segment's
rate by the combined segment sizes of both periods, then compare. If the
standardised direction differs from the raw direction, the mix shift is the finding.

## Writing the explanation

Three sentences, in this order:

1. The delta, and its split: "Pendapatan turun 12,4%, terdiri dari volume -8,1 poin,
   harga -3,0 poin, dan mix -1,3 poin, dengan residual 0,0."
2. The driver: "Penurunan terpusat pada dua produk, yang menyumbang 78% dari total
   penurunan."
3. The limit: "Data ini tidak memuat harga pesaing atau catatan promosi, jadi
   penyebab di luar penjualan tidak dapat diuji."

No sentence may name a cause that is not one of the computed components. Weather,
competitors, seasonality, and campaigns are outside this dataset unless a column
records them. Naming them is invention.

## Chart budget

At most two charts in F3.

| Chart | Worth drawing when |
|---|---|
| waterfall of the delta into components | always, if the components are non-zero |
| sorted bar of segment contribution | the driver is a segment, not a component |

The waterfall is the one chart in this run that a shop owner will understand at a
glance. Make it exact: the bars must add up to the printed delta, and the residual
bar is drawn even when it is zero.

## Prohibited in F3

- A decomposition whose residual is not printed, or not zero.
- A cause that is not a component of the decomposition.
- External causes: weather, competitors, holidays not in the data, campaigns not in
  the data.
- A ratio change quoted without numerator and denominator.
- Percentages of percentages, or a share of a share.
- Reporting a small segment as the driver because its percentage change is large.
- Correlating two columns and calling it a cause. F3 decomposes; it does not
  correlate.

## Exit check

All of these must be true before `advance_phase`:

- the delta restated, with the noise baseline printed
- decomposition level chosen and stated, with all components printed
- residual printed and zero, or the decomposition rejected
- EP per segment summing to 1.0, with a `lainnya` bucket and both sides reconciled
- denominator audit done if the metric is a ratio
- Simpson sign check done on the largest segments, with the outcome recorded
- explanation written in three sentences, with the limit named
- `evidence/F3.json` written, one entry per component and one for the driver
- `cannot_answer` updated if any part of the decomposition was not computable
